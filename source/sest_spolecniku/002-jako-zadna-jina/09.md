# **Get Lost, Jacob!**

“Let’s just say I’m not fond of the police,” Xavier replied calmly. “I’m always happy to bleed them a little, even on your behalf. By the way, I assume the charming Xantippa Miller told you about me.”

“Who else? She was completely hysterical, scared out of her mind. Why?”

“I heard your name for the first time when she accused me of being a provocateur sent after her by a certain Miss Lydia.”

“She didn’t mention that, but it doesn’t surprise me. Who else would she blame?”

Lydia clearly assumed Xavier understood her situation better than he actually did.

“I suspect she panicked the moment she realized who I am and what I do,” Xavier continued evenly.

“You frightened her quite a bit,” Lydia admitted. “You have a remarkable reputation, Mr. Valehart. But isn’t our **Asphalt‑Transit** beneath someone who seriously damaged the interests of **First Savings**?”

“I’ll disappoint you — a corporation is a corporation. But I’m glad you’re not underestimating me. Which is why I’ll ask you another question.”

“Go ahead.”

“Do you really believe I’d deserve my reputation if a simpleton like Xantippa Miller could figure out I was targeting the corporation she works for?”

Lydia wasn’t stupid. “You want me to believe you ran into her by accident, and by another accident you happened to be here today to save my life?”

Xavier shook his head.

“I didn’t save your life. You defended yourself well. And the moment you realized you could run, you’d have been out of danger. Those three bunglers couldn’t have hurt you.”

“That’s what *you* think,” Lydia said bitterly. “When I saw that knife, my mind went blank.”

By then everything was over. The police car was hauling away the perpetrators, along with witness statements. Only Jacob Lickboothe remained, too afraid to approach, too afraid to leave.

“Get lost, Jacob!” Lydia shouted. “I’m not in the mood to discuss your thesis today.”

“And tomorrow, Director?”

“Tomorrow neither,” Lydia said firmly. “Never again. Find someone else. We’re done, understand?”

She spoke calmly, without her earlier outrage.

“He should be grateful,” she added. “He never understood I’m not the kind of corporate princess who could or would help him climb the ladder. Did he really impress you?” she asked Xavier.

“It was delightful,” Xavier admitted openly. “He looked at me as if I were his god. Whatever nonsense I said, he wrote it down.”

Xavier made sure to speak in a way Lydia would enjoy. If she disliked Jacob, then a touch of irony at his expense had to please her.

But here he misstepped.

“You’re quite the professional,” Lydia said dryly. “Another minute and I’d believe you were my best friend. If I turned around now and claimed that timid boy isn’t so bad, what would you say?”

“That despite his flaws he has a bright future ahead,” Xavier replied without bothering to pretend he didn’t know what she meant. *These clever girls always end up fooling themselves,* he thought with disdain.

“And if I asked you to be honest with me. What then?”

“I’d tell you that if it weren’t for the box in your briefcase, I wouldn’t be wasting my time with you.”

That caught Lydia off guard. But her green fox‑eyes stayed focused, unshaken. Xavier began to feel the ground slipping under him. The euphoria he’d felt since morning — amplified by *Fiery Flame* and the lizard’s primitive presence — was fading. In its place came the familiar exhaustion of long‑term insomnia.

“You’re saying the only thing that interests you is my box?” Lydia asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Have you been inside?”

“No. I’ve had it for a week, but I didn’t feel like dealing with it. Why is it so important to you?”

There was a strange undertone in her voice. Xavier was almost certain he wasn’t the only one interested in Lydia’s ticket into the Game. *Who are the others?*

“You mean me specifically?” he asked.

“No, I mean you — the so‑called elite,” Lydia said seriously. “My father, my stepbrother, yesterday Xantippa Miller, and now you. I can’t imagine worse candidates for virtual entertainment. Aren’t you all terribly busy?” she added bitterly.

“I’m certainly not,” he assured her with a faint smile. He was struggling to stay focused. “My business has been going downhill lately.”

“I noticed,” Lydia said dryly. “Xantippa showed me your photos during her little hysterical episode. You looked like the last Plastic — healthy, though. Today you look chic, but half‑dead.”

“I’m perfectly fine.”

The little fairy appeared in a puff of mist and tapped his forehead with her wand. “You are absolutely *not* fine,” she warned. “My monitoring functions show your blood pressure dropping fast. You’ll collapse any moment. I ordered a taxi — say goodbye to your new friend and try to make it home.”

“Of course.”

“Of course *what*?” Lydia asked, studying him.

“I’m talking to my fairy,” he said, earning another incredulous stare.

“You’re hallucinating, Mr. Valehart?”

“No, that thing,” he pointed helplessly at his smart headband. The same situation he’d experienced in the Game repeated — losing the ability to articulate. Only the lizard’s primitive consciousness kept him upright; the higher parts of his brain were shutting down.

“Are you all right?” Lydia repeated. “Are you all right?”

No, he wasn’t all right — and he wasn’t fully conscious.

“Good animal, good animal, don’t bite, you beast,” he heard the fairy trying to negotiate with the lizard — and then nothing.

***

When he came to, he found himself in his own bed, though he had no idea how he got there. His smart headband wasn’t nearby, so he couldn’t ask the fairy. From the kitchen, behind the glass door, came the clatter of dishes — and with horror he realized someone was in his apartment.

This wasn’t his usual paranoia — this was the collapse of his last bastion. Xavier always lived alone and never allowed visitors. He met clients only on neutral ground, always keeping a polite, solid wall between himself and others.

*Who is that?* he thought. *How did I get here? Did the biometric locks fail?*

Someone had undressed him and neatly folded his jacket and tie on the wooden chair beside the bed. His black leather shoes lay beneath it.

*At least I’m not tied up,* he thought with relief, trying to move — only to slam his head back into the pillow. *Why tie me up when you can drug me?* he thought grimly.

At that moment the kitchen door opened and Lydia Carver walked into the bedroom. She must have just showered — her hair hung limp, droplets clinging to her golden skin. She was wearing Xavier’s bathrobe, the same one he’d worn yesterday when greeting the courier from the **Game Center**.

She held a small medical kit and was wrapping a bandage around her arm, where deep bite marks showed. Then she injected herself with a regenerative shot from Xavier’s supplies and looked around curiously.

“You’re awake?” she asked when she noticed him watching. “I thought you were done for. It’s almost six in the evening. Want something to drink?”

*Six in the evening? I must have collapsed before one. Nearly five hours gone? No wonder she made herself at home,* he thought, suppressing the disgust he felt at her intrusion.

“What happened?” he asked.

“I’m not entirely sure,” she shrugged. “When you started staggering, the taxi you ordered arrived. At that moment you… acted out a little.”

“Acted out?”

She showed her bitten arm. “Like an animal with rabies. If you hadn’t saved my life, I’d have left you on the street. Luckily you passed out quickly.”

“And how did we get here?”

“Your AI contacted mine. You have a very devoted fairy. Careful and attentive. You must have been lucky when you bought her.”

She spoke with the bitterness of someone who’d been cheated when buying her own headgear.

“I’m sorry I bit you,” he said lightly — as if civilized people occasionally bit others. He noticed Lydia raise an eyebrow but she didn’t pursue it. “How did you get past the biometric locks?”

“Guess.”

“No idea,” he shrugged, pretending not to care. “Tell me, please. I forbade my fairy access to the door locks.”

“Don’t be so tense.” Lydia studied him carefully.

*Body‑language expert,* he realized. The art of reading people came and went in fashion. It arrived with fanfare, promising absolute dominance over business partners. It left quietly, like everything that couldn’t deliver on its own hype.

> No one will ever lie to you again!

So proclaimed the bombastic flyers, usually decorated with the latest trends in psychology and AI. Important negotiations were therefore conducted without smart AI tools, leaving people dependent on their own abilities — which were usually worthless. But there were natural talents, rare white ravens, who could read people like books, especially with proper training.

If Lydia was one of them, Xavier had a problem.

“I have many enemies,” he explained. “Security is important to me. Could you please answer?”

“Security is important to me as well,” Lydia replied calmly, her green eyes fixed on him. “Someone tried to kill me today.”

“I had nothing to do with that.”

“Of course not,” she said. “If you had, you’d certainly tell me. An honorable man like you.”

She pulled out the AICHAT smart glasses Xavier had shoved into a drawer yesterday, not wanting to be reminded of the fairy’s mold‑green exorcism. He shuddered at the thought of the toxic green residue seeping through Lydia’s freshly washed fingers.

“Put that down!” he shouted. “Get that filth away from me!”
