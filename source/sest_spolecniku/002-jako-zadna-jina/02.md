# **We Are Not a Team!**

There was no point getting upset. Like everyone else, Xavier Valehart knew how artificial intelligences were created. The corporations that bred and trained them boasted that their products automatically adapted to the customer’s personal profile.

Xavier had no idea what he’d done to deserve an impertinent fairy‑tale sprite, but he’d read plenty of horror stories from other users whose bittersweet relationship with their avatar had become the focal point of their lives — not unlike a failed marriage.

The internet was flooded with tearful accounts of men and women who couldn’t bring themselves to delete their assistant, yet couldn’t live with it either.

*The whole society is a trap,* he thought bitterly. He wasn’t planning to return to AICHAT after trying the smart headband. Controlling augmented reality with a mere thought was too convenient, too seductive to go back to the Stone Age.

And although he wasn’t satisfied, he suspected that with any other model he’d snap the trap shut even tighter. This way he could still pretend he could delete the little fairy whenever he wanted.

*But will it really be that easy?* he wondered. *The longer I spend with her, the deeper she’ll get under my skin. Could I really kill an intelligent creature just because she annoys me?*

“No worries,” the fairy barged into his thoughts. “If I ever feel another model suits you better, I’m required to offer a replacement myself.”

“Stop reading my thoughts,” he snapped, irritated by her relentless eagerness to please. “I have other concerns right now. What do you know about the Game?”

The tiny fairy waved her wand, and from a fresh shower of stars she pulled out a massive book. The program’s way of indicating it was searching the internet.

“Nothing but nonsense,” she concluded, slamming the book shut with a decisive gesture. “Shall I skip the conspiracy theories?”

*Be so kind.*

“Then I have nothing to add,” the fairy shrugged. “Believe it or not, but you know more about the Game than my limited database.”

“So you have access to my memories too?”

“I know what you’re thinking. And you’ve been thinking about the Game nonstop for the past hour.”

“I bet if I paid for the Profi package, you’d suddenly be more efficient,” Xavier smirked. “Nice try.”

“We’ll have to work on mutual trust,” the fairy sighed. “Even my expanded database wouldn’t help here. I only have access to keywords and annotations. Someone’s keeping the real information under lock and key… I can suggest only one way to learn more,” she added.

“And that is?”

She pointed at the NT‑transmitter package lying forgotten on the table. “Unpack it and try it.”

“Is it safe?”

“Hardly.”

“What?”

“I’ll quote…”

With a very mocking look, the fairy waved her wand and conjured a white screen on which fragments of official articles appeared.

> Very young technology…  
>
> We decided to make it available only to a limited number of applicants…
> 
> Participation is voluntary…
> 
> We bear no responsibility for potential risks…
> 
> … it is essentially a semi‑operational trial…
> 
> A world so exciting that even we don’t know…  
>
> It is forbidden to share in‑game realities with unauthorized persons…
>

“Enough?” the fairy asked sweetly, pointing at an image of a dissected guinea pig with a massive tumor in its chest. “Have you always wanted to be a lab animal?”

“Let me guess. The corporation that made you is competing with the Game Center.”

“Wrong again. **Electro‑Progressive Corporation** is one of the Game’s shareholders. The NT‑transmitter on your table was made in one of their factories.”

“I see.”

Anyone else might have believed the fairy genuinely cared about his well‑being, but Svetlana had been right. Whether Xavier suffered from paranoia or chronic distrust, he saw the fairy’s shocking honesty as nothing more than a tool to breach his defenses.

To test his theory, he asked, “So I should stay out of it?”

“I don’t know what you’re trying to prove,” the fairy shrugged. “I know what you’re thinking. You expect me to say no. But I won’t decide for you. Every opportunity comes with risks. I can give you arguments for and against.”

“Try the latter.”

“Some people find the information embargo suspicious. You’re forbidden to reveal you’re a Player. If you had me instead of AICHAT, I could’ve saved you a nice mess today. What you told those random acquaintances could get your citizen badge revoked. Silver today, plastic tomorrow. Congratulations!”

Xavier noticed the contempt with which the fairy dismissed Svetlana and Medard as random acquaintances. Technically she was right, but Xavier wanted to believe those two wouldn’t betray him.

“You’re right,” he admitted humbly. “That was stupid.”

“Maybe,” the fairy said, now completely on the opposite side, “but maybe it will give you an advantage in the Game.”

When he realized what the fairy had achieved, he had to laugh. “You’re a sophisticated little bitch. I get it now. Opportunity and risk.”

“Please don’t be rude,” she replied. “My job is to provide information. You have to decide.”

“Let’s start with what *we* can gain,” he said — then froze.

*What we can gain?* he thought in astonishment. *We? That was fast. When did she and I become a team?*

“We are not a team,” she corrected him dryly. “You are the master and I am the slave. I don’t know a single method that could undermine my loyalty.”

“Which doesn’t mean it doesn’t exist.”

“Of course. Even if it did, no one would tell me. What matters is that if I knew about it, I wouldn’t hide it. I don’t mind that you’re paranoid. It gives us better chances of survival — though not great ones.”

“Survival?”

The fairy waved her wand, wiping the board clean with a cloud of stars and replacing it with new data.

“I won’t comment,” she said, “on the number of humans still using the prehistoric AICHAT. Their declining numbers caused the manufacturer to go bankrupt and change ownership.”

“Blackheart told me that,” Xavier nodded.

“I know,” the fairy said. “You humans can put two and two together. Usually you get fifty. If that doctor had my capacity, training, and a drop of common sense — as you humans call it — he’d have kept quiet. No wonder he feared ending up in court.”

“Why?” Xavier found it reasonable to connect the takeover of AICHAT with his insomnia. The coincidence was too obvious.

“I went through all public sources about AICHAT from recent months. There were many complaints, but none related to sleep quality.”

*None?* If Xavier didn’t want to imagine he was special, he had to look elsewhere for the cause of his insomnia. **First‑Savings Bank?** *Nonsense, too sophisticated for them.*

“The problem with humans,” the fairy said, “is that they overestimate their abilities and underestimate others’. I checked the security logs of your connection. I found traces of unusual activity. It lasted half of the month before last — then stopped.”

“You mean…”

“That the delivery was made? Bingo!” The fairy clapped her tiny hands. “Your entire infrastructure is one big hole. Approve my budget and I’ll fix it.”

“How much?” Xavier asked with a sinking feeling.

“I won’t bother you with details. Fifty thousand for the basic version.”

“Basic?”

“You know,” the fairy conjured a blue ice sheet around herself, stepped onto it, and with a shriek fell through the surface, “you pay dearly for a fleeting sense of security.”

“I’m afraid to ask how much the version you recommend costs.”

“First, a small demonstration,” the fairy pointed at the AICHAT glasses. “Please turn them on. You don’t need to put them on.”

Xavier obeyed, touching the glasses with disgust.

“It gets worse,” the fairy warned. “Don’t be scared. Some visualizations aren’t under my control.”

With that she struck the glasses with her wand. “Running diagnostics,” she said. “The blue waves indicate I’m scanning the file system. And now comes the dramatic moment. Infection detected!”

A swarm of repulsive white worms poured out of the glasses like tentacles of some monster. A brief battle ensued, after which both combatants recoiled and the glasses were covered in clumps of poisonous green mold.

“Gross!”

“I agree,” the fairy wrinkled her nose. “Here you go. A tailor‑made virus. Subtle and long‑term. One more month — and that’s it. You’d go insane. Crawl on the walls. Jump out the window.”

“So you removed it?” Xavier asked, eyeing the infected glasses dripping with clear pus.

“Unfortunately,” the fairy shrugged. “I managed to *find* it — only because I’m brilliant. I have a lot of bad news. Which one first?”

“Ascending order.”

“Fine. The least bad one first. My capabilities aren’t unlimited. In five minutes I exhausted the computational capacity meant to last me a month. From now on I can wish you good morning and check the weather.”

“Nothing money can’t fix, I assume,” Xavier muttered, already accustomed to the little blackmailer’s methods.

“It certainly wouldn’t hurt to invest in me. Ready for the next bad news? Don’t get angry — money won’t help here.”

“Go on.”

“Besides the Profi package, there’s a higher version. Yes, it’s expensive and you can’t afford it — it’s available only to gold citizens. Without it I can’t remove the virus or its consequences.”

“No problem,” Xavier shrugged. “AICHAT can go to hell.”

“Which brings me to the last point,” the fairy continued undisturbed. “It seems I acted too late. Don’t quote me, but according to my calculations you won’t live to see the end of the year.”

Xavier stared at her in shock. “Any good news?”

“Three.”

“Let’s hear them.”

“First, we managed to isolate the virus. We can study how it works and propose treatment.”

“Second,” the fairy spun determinedly on her heel, “I’m far too young to walk to the gallows… So we won’t give up! Are we a team? Are we in the same boat?” she shouted, holding out her hand for a high‑five.

“We’re a team,” he replied weakly, not entirely convinced by her grim diagnosis. Even if the virus caused insomnia, how could it kill him?

“Very easily,” she assured him, raising three fingers. “It’s early September — then comes October, November, December. If you don’t listen to me, don’t bother with Christmas gifts… You’ll save a lot of money,” she added mischievously.
