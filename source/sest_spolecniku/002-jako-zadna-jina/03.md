# **Don’t Tell Me I Turned Into a Woman?**

It was time to log into the Game. As for the little fairy blackmailer and her ominous warnings, Xavier Valehart decided to postpone all of that. He had no doubt the AI in the smart headband had been designed to whip him toward higher social performance.

It was a mutually beneficial deal. The more he earned, the more ended up in the corporation’s pocket. And there was no doubt that living under the shadow of death could motivate anyone.

Maybe there *were* consequences of that unknown computer virus, and maybe there *was* a chance he’d die before the end of the year — but it was just as likely the fairy was exaggerating to push him into investing in her development.

*Whatever happens, happens,* he thought with a touch of fatalism as he lay down in bed. The green LED on the nightstand blinked soothingly while a mild dizziness overtook him — the same panic‑like sensation he’d felt in the hallway of the Game Center. The ground seemed to drop away beneath him, and the ceiling descended like the herald of a claustrophobic attack.

*No use crying over spilled milk,* he thought as he closed his eyes and drifted off, feeling his body slip out of his control.

Blue sky overhead…

He woke in an unfamiliar place, beneath the wide crown of a tree through which morning sunlight filtered. Around him grew a dense forest with massive trunks covered in soft moss and mushroom caps. Somewhere in the distance came the sound of water, punctuated by the cries of gulls.

“Green hell!”

He lay wedged in a fork of branches, tied with a rope to a thick limb, his feet braced against a hollow in the rough bark. When he moved, he nearly fell into a five‑meter drop.

His heart hammered wildly. Once he calmed down, he realized whoever had put him there hadn’t meant harm. The rope was meant to keep him from falling while asleep.

*Everyone has their own idea of comfort,* he thought as he untied himself.

*Is this the Game? If so, it’s like nothing else.*

This wasn’t virtual reality — it was real. When his fingers worked the knots, he felt the coarse fibers pressing into the soft pads of his fingertips.

It wasn’t just the deceived senses that stunned him — it was the indescribable euphoria. The gray curtains and helplessness caused by insomnia were gone; instead he overflowed with energy like a freshly rested child.

*Wherever I am,* he realized with terrifying clarity, *going back to where I came from will be agony.*

A quick inspection confirmed his body had grown younger; the scars on his legs were gone. And though he couldn’t see his face, he felt no trace of his usual stubble — only remarkably smooth skin, with almost feminine qualities.

*Don’t tell me I turned into a woman,* he thought, quickly patting his chest and between his thighs. *No — nothing extra, nothing missing.*

He didn’t feel like he was in a virtual game, nor like he was dreaming. He was in a foreign body, in a foreign place. There was no friendly guide, no tutorial to teach him basic skills — not even a user interface.

He looked around. As a city man, the deep forest and its sounds frightened him; he couldn’t orient himself, and he suspected no one slept in treetops for fun.

*I mustn’t rush,* he reasoned. Instinctive fear of wild animals kept him frozen in place.

*Why the wilderness? I know nothing about surviving in nature.*

He was a professional troublemaker — he launched covert and overt attacks on corporations and got paid for it. He knew legal loopholes, silver and gold habits, the weaknesses of corporate managers, and he could win most duels. But he doubted he could hunt a deer. The farthest he’d ventured into nature was a city park and a half‑forgotten military exercise.

*But it seems I’m not here by accident.*

He examined his clothing — a remarkable material that camouflaged itself like a chameleon against the bark and leaves. It looked like camouflage fabric but felt like scaled leather.

*And what’s this?*

On the gloves of camouflaged leather, a ring of matte metal with a massive red stone stood out like a fist in the eye.

When he touched it, a two‑dimensional wall appeared. Had he not been firmly braced in the branches, he would have fallen — this time for real — because he had unwisely untied the safety rope.

*A multidimensional space?*

In virtual games, magical items often served as storage, but intangible virtual reality could perform miracles that were problematic in the real world.

As far as he could tell, the two‑dimensional panel served as an entrance to three shelves covered with items: food, water bottles, red stones, gray crystals, and weapons.

Several questions presented themselves: What happens if there isn’t enough space for the wall to unfold? Could the wall collapse and slice his wrist in half? Did the ring need a power source? Were magical items powered by something — or did they run on goodwill alone?

In virtual reality, where consequences don’t exist, people can experiment freely. But if you have a physical body — and pain as the inevitable consequence of reckless experimentation — courage evaporates quickly, assuming you have even a shred of self‑preservation.

The initial excitement slowly gave way to creeping dread. *Why is the Game Center testing Players for pain? Certainly not because the World they send us into is meant to be fun and harmless.*

Remembering how easily he had revealed Game Center secrets, he felt a pang of guilt. Even if he had no illusions about the society he lived in, who could guarantee the Game was any better? *How do I get out of here?* he wondered. *How will Medard or Svetlana manage?*

VRAU!

A terrifying roar echoed through the forest, followed by silence. A herd of animals rushed beneath the tree — creatures resembling wild boars with long tusks. In their wake came a beast with a brown lizard‑like body and scaly skin. It had a long snout with short sharp teeth, legs with powerful claws, and a sturdy tail.

It managed to catch one of the boars, which had unfortunately fallen into a pit and tumbled several times. The lizard seized it in its jaws without hesitation. There was no struggle — only a brief flash of blue light, after which the boar froze, eyes bulging, watching helplessly as the lizard tore open its soft belly.

When the creature finally exhaled its last breath, it vanished from the world, replaced by a single red stone and a single gray crystal. The lizard swallowed both greedily without chewing, then lifted its snout and tasted the air with a forked tongue.

*Can it smell me?*

Instead of fear, Xavier felt excitement. While the deep forest and unfamiliar surroundings filled him with dread, the monster beneath the tree represented a tangible target — something he could fight. From the multidimensional space he pulled out a long fang, a knife with a blade the length of a man’s forearm, set with a red stone in the hilt.

As soon as he gripped the handle, a faint tingling shot through his fingers, not unlike an electric current. When it faded, he felt as though the knife were growing into the flesh of his palm, connecting to his capillaries and nerves. It took considerable effort not to drop the weapon.

*Damn magical tools,* he thought, pressing the ring in hopes of closing the multidimensional space. It vanished as quietly as it had appeared. *They could’ve at least given me a manual!*

The lizard still sniffed the soft moss, drawn by Xavier’s scent. Whether it could climb or not no longer mattered. As a city man, Xavier had never tasted the lure of the hunt, yet he longed to test himself against the creature, still under the influence of his initial euphoria.

*I’m not being rational,* he realized as he carefully climbed down. *I should stop, assess the situation, and avoid unnecessary risk.* But a primal force pushed him forward. The society he lived in was bound by habits and rules; duels were too scripted to allow true randomness.

*I’m an adrenaline‑pumped idiot!* With that thought he jumped and drove the fang behind the lizard’s head. The blade struck the spine, slid sideways, and pierced the thick neck. The lizard thrashed, and its powerful tail struck Xavier’s legs, knocking him to the ground — right next to the snapping jaws, where the same blue light appeared that had paralyzed the boar.

Then it was over. It didn’t take long for the dead body to vanish, replaced by a red stone and a gray crystal. Even the blood on the fang gradually disappeared.

Now that he could observe the transformation up close, Xavier noticed that the creature’s particles partly dispersed into the surroundings and partly condensed into the red stone and gray crystal.

*Should I eat them too?* he wondered. *Or keep them and sell them later?*

In most virtual games, monsters dropped items, but here — in a physical environment — such a mechanism felt almost inappropriate, like a denial of common sense. A Player couldn’t know whether the laws of physics or metaphysics applied.

He could consider the fang a magical tool or a product of advanced technology, but he had no explanation for why a living creature vanished upon death, replaced by stones.

*Who would send us here without a shred of guidance?* Xavier wondered. There was a legally enforced information embargo that made no sense. Dr. Blackheart, an employee of the Game Center, had wanted access to Game information — risking his corporate career. Why? Shouldn’t he be the one closest to the source?

*Even if I believed that those who run the Game don’t understand it themselves, I’m certainly not the first to enter this World. These red stones and gray crystals mean something, and I doubt no one knows their purpose.*

Under normal circumstances, the internet would be full of guides on how to progress in the Game, but all influencers — powerful or not — were silent or tried to read meaning into the scraps of official statements. No Player had publicly broken the Game Center contract yet, which was more than surprising in a society where information leaks were routine.

Xavier built several working hypotheses and gradually disproved them. Perhaps each Player’s experience was unique and non‑transferable. But then there would be no reason to hide anything.

It wasn’t about the embargo itself — but about the willingness of participants to obey it voluntarily. And if the Game Center was a joint corporate venture, and the Game contained secrets no one wanted to share, why would corporations bother making the Game accessible to the public at all?

All available facts suggested the Game Center had little interest in letting people like Xavier or Medard inside — or in letting lower‑tier employees like Dr. Blackheart know more than necessary.

Xavier leaned toward the idea that some force had compelled the corporations to open the Game to the world — which they did with absurd obstacles to discourage potential applicants. And if those applicants succeeded, like Xavier or Medard, the information embargo ensured they would either fail or progress at a snail’s pace.

And if an independent Player persisted despite everything, corporations planned to secure him by offering a bronze or silver badge — as Dr. Blackheart had hinted.

*I’m becoming a decent conspirator,* Xavier thought with a smile. *But this theory suits me.*

A man like him, who hated the corporate sphere with all his heart, welcomed any chance to oppose it. In the real world he had little hope of success. Here, on the other hand — if his theory was correct — he found himself in a favorable starting position.

It was time for a small experiment.
