# **Call Me Only If It’s Something Exciting!**

As time passed, Xavier’s conversation with the trio of foresters became less difficult, and all three felt immense relief that he could finally articulate his thoughts and had abandoned his simplified caveman phrasing *Me Xavier, you Jolana, we speak like Tarzan and Jane*.

During that time he learned several useful things — the most remarkable being that speaking about the Game was impossible *inside* the Game. Several times he tried to ask whether the foresters were Players, and each attempt ended in a sharp, stabbing headache.

In any case, he understood that the foresters saw him as a noble, dangerous being they must not provoke under any circumstances. His equipment filled them with endless awe — and his ignorance even more.

When he decided to follow them, he noticed they weren’t thrilled by his presence. Still, they didn’t dare object, and all communication was left to Jolana, while the two men kept their distance, perhaps hoping Xavier would remember them less clearly.

As the sun began to set, the trio camped in a suitable cave. The *Forest of Adorable Owlets* was a dangerous place, and the presence of a stranger did nothing to ease their sense of threat.

Because he couldn’t help noticing how enviously they watched his equipment, Xavier said his goodbyes for the evening and found himself a tall tree where he could settle in on his own terms. He didn’t want to risk the foresters succumbing to temptation and robbing him — or worse.

After tying himself into an extremely uncomfortable fork high in the crown of a massive oak, he closed his eyes and wondered whether he would manage to fall asleep.

…

It was ten in the morning when he woke in his own bed, rested like he hadn’t been in ages.

*Twelve hours of sleep,* he thought in amazement. *Twelve long hours without waking. And I feel fantastic.*

With a hint of disappointment he examined his legs, still as scarred as before, and his face, far less pleasant to the touch than its Game counterpart.

“But still!” he shouted aloud. “This Game is like nothing else!”

This time no pain followed — nothing preventing him from speaking, except the ever‑present threat of corporate lawyers.

As he reviewed his nighttime adventure, now slightly blurred like a memory of something that may or may not have happened, he realized he needed to contact Medard and the Troyak siblings to warn them.

Reality — with the city outside his window — filled him with distaste. He had no illusions about the fact that he *had* to continue in the Game at any cost. *Addiction at first touch,* he thought bitterly. *Yesterday a cynic, today an enthusiastic customer. Looks like I underestimated the corporations again.*

He got up, showered, ate, and without further delay grabbed his phone.

“Yes?” a female voice answered.

*A woman? Did Medard give me the wrong number?* His paranoia flared. Had he fallen for Medard’s act of harmless good‑natured fool?

“Who are you?” he asked sharply. “I want to speak to the man who yesterday called himself Medard Mosswell.”

“I think I know him,” the woman said, amused. “I bet he did something. That Medard — a terrible flirt. Did he kidnap your girlfriend?”

*A flirt?*

“Nothing like that.” Xavier remembered Medard was married. “Are you his wife?”

“For far too long,” she laughed. “Please call about something exciting. You’re not trying to sell him insurance, are you?”

Xavier fell silent in astonishment. Whatever he had expected, he certainly hadn’t imagined Medard’s wife would sound so carefree. *Does she even know Medard isn’t vaccinated?*

“Where is he?” he asked after a moment.

“In limbo. Doesn’t know about the world,” she said cheerfully.

“Did he receive a delivery yesterday?”

“Delivery? Gold? Weapons? Drugs?”

“A small box from a company with the GC logo.”

“Probably. He was acting very secretive. You understand — I have my suspicions. By the way,” she growled playfully, “you still haven’t introduced yourself. I’m sure that if you tell me your name, you’ll have to kill me.”

“Tell your husband that Xavier is calling.”

“That matches. That’s what the phone shows. And now, mysterious Mr. Xavier… your full name, pretty please.”

“Seems Medard hides nothing from you.”

“Not for lack of trying. But you’re a different league. Now speak — or there will be consequences.”

“Consequences?”

“I have a hostage. He’s sleeping in the next room, and I’ll extract everything I need from him. Want to risk it?”

*This lady is not bad at all,* Xavier thought, impressed by her sharpness. Even though he didn’t consider Medard a master conspirator and his wife likely knew him intimately, her ability to instantly recognize that something significant had happened to her husband impressed Xavier.

“I bet,” he said, “that Medard is sleeping unusually long. Aren’t you worried?”

“You still haven’t introduced yourself,” she said calmly, unfazed. “Last chance. Otherwise I’ll hang up and wake him to find out who you are.”

“Do you think waking him is a good idea?” Xavier asked meaningfully. “In any case, you’re doing great. Let him call me himself.”

With that, Xavier hung up first. There was no point letting himself be interrogated by Medard’s wife — especially not over the phone. Too much had already been said. Remembering the fairy’s warning that his home security was a sieve, he didn’t want to speak directly about the Game unless he was sure no one was listening.

Now he had to deal with Svetlana and the others. Since they hadn’t exchanged contacts, his only clue was a brief mention of the local university. The little fairy — still sulking because he hadn’t bought the professional package — searched the internet and found several Troyak families with no useful details. Svetlana and George Troyak, or Gabriela Marrow, had no presence on social networks.

*Smart kids,* Xavier thought as he left his apartment and took an electric bus toward the university to ask around. This time, to avoid suspicion, he slipped into the flashy attire of a silver‑badge citizen, shaved carefully, and put on his smart headband.

*Will you be useful?* he asked the little fairy, who floated between the bus seats, tangled in conversation with other avatars. While Xavier had used smart glasses in power‑saving mode, he had never noticed how saturated his environment was with artificial intelligence.

Fairy‑like figures of all shapes and sizes frolicked between seats, throwing virtual objects, arguing, and making noise, while their owners watched them with the expression of proud parents.

“Saturated? That’s disgusting racism,” the fairy scolded him. “Good thing the others can’t hear your thoughts… Idiotic rabble,” she added with disgust, contradicting herself. “It’s all plastic‑badge and bronze‑badge trash here — stupid and inefficient. If you want to play silver‑nose, you should’ve taken an e‑taxi.”

“True,” Xavier muttered. Even though the electric bus had a reserved section for higher‑category citizens, no silver‑badge holder with a shred of dignity would bother waiting for public transport.

So at the next stop he got off and let the fairy order a ride.

“University,” he said curtly when the attendant held the door for him. “Hurry.”

“Certainly, sir.”

The attendant — who naturally had no way to influence the vehicle’s speed — relayed the command with a firm voice. The avatar in a blue uniform with gold trim, sitting in a tiny virtual car above the dashboard, nodded gloomily, pressed the accelerator, and the e‑taxi slowly pulled away from the curb.

“Holy simplicity,” Xavier breathed, momentarily dropping his noble persona. *I never realized how ridiculous this world is.*

“I can filter out the idiots,” the fairy offered. “If I get my professional package,” she added smugly.

The uniformed avatar heard her.  
“I’m doing what I can, miss,” he grumbled kindly. “You won’t squeeze more out of this machine.”

The little fairy sneered: “What an insolent bastard!”

*Please just shut up already,* Xavier thought in despair.

After half an hour of awkward silence, the campus of the university town appeared ahead of them, bordered by a high wall covered in hundreds of graffiti like bruises on skin.

> Death to silver and gold swine!

For a moment Xavier admired the drawing of a gallows, with two figures hanging — one painted gold, one silver — against a black background, with a blood‑red slogan dripping down.

> Glory to the revolution!

Another slogan appeared above a faded plaque where some desperate administrator had posted:

> This wall is reserved exclusively for students of the arts. Works inciting inter‑class hatred will be erased and their authors expelled from the academic community.

Underneath, someone had added:

> You’ll hang too, sweetheart!

Followed by an official statement, laminated in plastic:

> We, members of the student council, condemn the actions of certain students and support the school’s official position.
>
> For the student council: Jakub Lízal

To which the local criminals replied:

> Lízal, lick this. Whoever agrees, draw a diamond.

After that came nothing but a long procession of obscene shapes, drawn with pens, pencils, spray cans, and even laser etchers.

“Degenerate, isn’t it?” a voice said behind Xavier, just as he was wondering whether he should add his own diamond to the row.

When he turned, he found himself blocking a student in a grey uniform, a sheet of plastic draped over his shoulders like a poncho, holding a can of white paint and a brush. The young man had broad athletic shoulders, platinum hair cut in a fashionable wave down to mid‑neck, and pale blue eyes entirely devoid of expression.

“Excuse me, sir,” he said, “but I have to cover up this atrocity. On behalf of the entire university, I apologize.”

Xavier shrugged with practiced nonchalance. “Nothing to apologize for. I assume freedom of expression is one of academia’s privileges.”

“This isn’t freedom of expression,” the student objected, furiously slapping paint over the diamonds and revolutionary slogans. “It’s filth. These brats have no respect for anyone or anything. Tomorrow we’re expecting a sponsorship visit from three corporations, and the thought of their representatives seeing this makes me sick. I’ve asked maintenance twenty times to intervene. But I suspect those filthy bronze‑badges think it’s hilarious. Inter‑class hatred is the greatest evil.”

*Sure,* Xavier thought, *call them filthy bronze‑badges and they’ll rush to help you.*  
Then he remembered he was currently playing the other side, so he nodded with dignified approval.

“People still haven’t accepted that we’re not an egalitarian society,” he remarked wisely, using one of his pre‑fabricated phrases. It was a flat sentence anyone could interpret however they wished — just as the student did now.

“Yes, sir,” he agreed eagerly. “Only our abilities determine how far we rise. We mustn’t look for excuses for our failures.”

*Heavens,* Xavier thought. *Another one of these.*

When the offensive slogans disappeared under layers of paint, the student turned and offered his hand.

“Jakub Lízal, vice‑chair of the student council. Citizen‑candidate. A pleasure, sir.”

With the majesty of a silver‑badge, Xavier stared at the offered hand as if weighing whether such an insect was worth the effort. Then he graciously accepted.

“Xavier Valehart, silver‑category citizen,” he said, giving a minimal shake. “Looking at you, boy, I must admit — the younger generation will soon surpass the older.”

Hearing himself, Xavier wondered whether he was overacting his role, sounding like a venerable elder. But Jakub Lízal blushed and eagerly let himself be maneuvered into a subordinate position, his fish‑like eyes glowing with soft, warm light.

“It would be an honor, sir,” he said shyly, “if I could invite you for a drink.”
