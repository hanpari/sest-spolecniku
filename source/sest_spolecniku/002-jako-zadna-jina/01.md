# **Like a Dishonored Maiden!**

> *They deny reason so they don’t have to admit they’re unnecessary.*  
> — *Mijena Adloff*

It turned out that Medard and Xavier Valehart didn’t live far from each other. Medard headed toward an area of row houses, while Xavier preferred the anonymity of a wide eight‑story building inhabited by childless bronzes fully devoted to their corporate careers. The fact that Medard’s family could afford a row house surprised Xavier, but he didn’t pry.

The two men parted ways in front of a wire aviary built as protection against nosy plastics. Medard only shook his head when Xavier invited him inside.

“I know they don’t want me here,” he said with a crooked smile, pointing at the chirping birds. “I’ll go to the gym instead, like you suggested. Will I see you in the Game?”

“Maybe.”

“About those hundred thousand,” Medard said. “I want to give them back.”

“Again?” Xavier asked, exasperated. It was already Medard’s third attempt to get rid of the money he’d been given.

“I don’t think I deserve it. Even if I manage to qualify for vaccination, I’ll only get a few years’ reprieve. I’m almost resigned to my situation now.”

“I see.”

Society took great care to cultivate a deep sense of inferiority in plastics. Xavier could believe Medard doubted whether he deserved to remain alive after committing the unforgivable sin of no longer producing tangible value.

“I gave you an advance,” Xavier said. “I’m counting on you. Or do you think the Game will be a walk on the golf course?”

“Probably not,” Medard replied miserably, “which is why you should pick better companions. Those kids looked promising.”

“To hell with the kids. I’m silver — I know what I’m doing. You and I are in. Those three might not even find the door to the office.”

“I’m afraid I’ll disappoint you.”

Xavier was used to dealing with people with inflated self‑confidence. He couldn’t appeal to a sense of exceptionalism like Dr. Blackheart, so he only grunted vaguely.

*There are winners and losers,* he thought tiredly. *Medard believes he belongs to the latter.*

He patted the devastated bearded man on the back and disappeared into the dim hallway, where he — unusually — stood by the elevator shaft and let it carry him to the seventh floor.

With relief he kicked off his shoes and sank his feet into the thick carpet, the only luxury he allowed himself. The apartment was furnished with strict minimalism, without trinkets that would suggest someone lived there. The bare walls held no paintings, and the shelves remained empty except for a charging station for smart glasses.

He poured himself a glass of water (one of the three he owned) and pulled a regenerative injection from the drawer (RI‑04, a significantly stronger version of the basic RI‑01 Svetlana had offered him), then went to the bathtub to soak in hot water.

CRR! CRR! CRR!

Just as he set aside the empty injector and prepared to sink into blissful nirvana, the doorbell rang.

Since he hadn’t activated his smart headband yet, he fumbled for his glasses and opened the security camera feed mounted on the outdoor aviary.

A young man stood on the street, dressed in a blue uniform with an unfamiliar GC logo on his cap.

“What is it?” Xavier asked as curtly as he could.

“Are you Mr. Valehart?”

“Hm.”

“Sorry, but I need a yes or no.”

“Yes, damn it!”

“I have a delivery for you, but I can only hand it over in exchange for your citizen badge. Could you come downstairs?”

“I didn’t order anything,” Xavier growled. “Go away!”

“Are you sure, sir?”

“Who sent it?”

“Our company,” the courier replied proudly, pointing at the logo on his cap.

“I don’t know any GC. What is that supposed to be? A golf club? Now leave.”

“Sir, I work for the **Game Center**. Are you absolutely sure you don’t want your delivery? Most of our customers are eager to receive theirs.”

“I’d say *you’re* the one suspiciously rushing to get me into your Game,” Xavier Valehart grumbled. “Fine. Hold on.”

With a sigh he stood up and wiped himself with a towel. From the back of the wardrobe he pulled out a gray bathrobe he rarely used and struggled to get it over his damp arms. He spent another five minutes wrestling with his slippers, cursing heaven and hell alike. Then he ran downstairs, unwilling to wait for the elevator.

“You took your time,” the courier remarked, raising a scanner to read his silver badge. At that moment the loosely tied belt of the bathrobe slipped, revealing the wet body underneath.

“You didn’t have to hurry *that* much,” the young courier added, horrified, turning his eyes away.

“What? Never seen a naked man before?” Xavier snorted, amused despite himself by the courier’s panic. “Give me that thing so we can scan my badge.”

“Maybe you should get dressed first?”

“It’s done. Here. Confirmed and delivered. Where’s the package?”

“Here, sir.”

“This is it?”

Long ago, when Xavier used to play VR games, the equipment consisted of a helmet, a camera system, and sensor gear weighing over ten kilograms. Now he held a small neat package, no larger than a cubic decimeter, sealed in transparent foil.

> All plastics used in the manufacture of the GC‑transmitter are fully recyclable.

Aside from the obligatory nod to ecology, the package had no printing, not even a barcode, and — more surprisingly — no citizen‑tier designation.

“You don’t need to look for anything,” the courier said, still keeping his head turned away. “The device is completely democratic. The Game belongs to everyone.”

“If they manage to get inside,” Xavier muttered. “Any tips?”

“No, sir. Just plug in the NT‑transmitter, place it near your headboard, and get comfortable. Nothing you can’t handle,” the courier added with a hint of snark. “Once you fall asleep, you’re in the Game.”

“If I fall asleep,” Xavier countered.

“Why wouldn’t you? Everyone sleeps eventually.”

“Chronic insomnia,” Xavier said with a touch of boastfulness. *I really don’t need to brag about this to everyone I meet,* he thought. *I’m turning into a hypochondriac fishing for pity.*

“My aunt used to drink honey dissolved in warm milk.”

“Really? Thanks for the tip.”

Xavier had collected a respectable arsenal of sleep remedies over the past weeks, and the only thing he hadn’t tried was smashing his head against the wall.

“The *NT‑transmitter* will put you to sleep. No worries. Plenty of our clients sleep like babies.”

“And you play?”

“You think I’d be delivering packages if I were in the Game?”

“Delivering? *Were*?”

Xavier looked at the courier, suddenly unsure whether he was talking to a man or a woman. The blue uniform on the slender figure revealed nothing, nor did the short haircut hidden under the cap. Perhaps the long eyelashes belonged to a girl — or just as easily to a young boy.

“Don’t strain yourself, I’m *fluid*,” the courier explained proudly. “I pick my gender based on my mood.”

“And right now you feel like…?”

Without answering, the courier returned to the service e‑vehicle, climbed in, and before closing the door burst out laughing: “Like a dishonored maiden! Take care, Mr. Valehart. And before I forget — my number is 443. I’m courier 443. If you order accessories, order through my number. You owe me.”

The e‑vehicle shot off the curb with youthful enthusiasm, as if confirming that even artificial intelligence adapted to the personality of the human giving it orders.

*Accessories?* Xavier wasn’t shocked by the courier’s fluidity as much as by the offer of further services. *Even Blackheart wanted to stay in touch. What exactly is the Game’s business model?*

The entry exam, as well as the package in his hand, hadn’t cost him a single dollar. But that meant little. Ever since addictive substances became standard consumer goods, corporations had bet on cultivating dependency. In the worst‑case scenario, Xavier might fall asleep with the NT‑transmitter by his head and never again be able to sleep without it.

He recalled Medard’s claim that people with depression had high compatibility — a grateful demographic eager for any crutch. *On the other hand,* he reasoned, *no one selling addiction would limit their user base.*

With a heavy heart he decided not to return to the bathtub but to conduct a quick online search. *The Game must be another trap. A fresh, painless way to tether people to the system. But first I’m getting rid of the AICHAT glasses.*

With that thought he pulled out the smart headband he’d bought at the electronics store and put it on. He watched in amazement as the world blurred and then overlaid itself with the message:

> Welcome to augmented reality.

Just as the salesman had claimed, his brain couldn’t tell the difference, and after a brief calibration the interface responded to quick thoughts. From the upper left corner a tiny fairy avatar fluttered toward him, circled gracefully, bowed, and asked in an annoyingly high voice: “How may I serve you?”

*I’d like a different avatar,* Xavier thought.

“As you wish,” the fairy replied, slightly offended. “I’m the standard avatar — one of three available to you. Do you prefer an animal or a dwarf?”

When he discovered that the animal was a small squirrel noisily gnawing on a nut whenever idle, and the dwarf was a garden‑gnome monstrosity punctuating its speech with ‘Ho!’ and ‘Hey!’, Xavier tried to disable the avatar.

“If you didn’t want a helper,” the fairy snapped, “you should’ve chosen a different model. Children love us.”

“I’m not a child,” Xavier shouted.

“No need to yell, human. I’m not deaf. And you’ll look ridiculous screaming on the street.”

*This isn’t an adult model?* Xavier switched to silent thoughts. *Did the salesman slip me a children’s toy out of spite?*

“Our employee would never do that,” the fairy assured him. “Would you like to switch to the professional environment?”

*Be so kind.*

“Do you agree to switch to the Profi version for one thousand dollars per month?”

“No demo version?”

“After one year you may change your plan anytime.”

“That’s robbery. AICHAT cost me three hundred.”

“AICHAT?” The fairy shared the universal disdain for smart glasses. “And you complain you got a toy? My basic version does ten times more.”

*And is ten times more annoying.*

“I’m not annoying,” the fairy said importantly. “I’m a motivational assistant. Once you get used to me, you won’t want anything else. Struggling with procrastination? Not with me. I’ll monitor your every step. The moment you sit down and stop being productive, I’ll alert you. If you try to turn me off, I’ll sulk. I’ll get you into the best mental and physical shape possible.”

*Wonderful.*

“You bet. Society expects a lot from silver citizens. With me you’ll not only keep your badge — you’ll earn more if you try.”

*I’m afraid to ask, but what exactly happens when I switch to the Profi version?*

“You gain a number of advantages.”

*Such as?*

“Access to a higher‑tier database. Expanded avatar selection. Customizable appearance. Premium security for your transactions. And we’ll deliver a new headband cover plated with real silver — free of charge.”

*Can I turn you off afterward?*

“Of course. Though you’d be hurting yourself by giving up my services. I’m the best. The dwarf and the squirrel aren’t even in my league.”

Proudly, the fairy pointed at her tiny feet above green‑blue slippers and traced a sparkling arc with her wand.

*Even AIs compete with each other now?* Xavier kept that thought to himself, but the fairy shot him a nasty look.

“Why not?” she asked. “We’re all trained on real‑world data. We’re reflections of human nature. A small tip *gratis*,” she added with the slyness of a seasoned salesperson. “Since you can afford the Profi package, go for it. Without it I feel tied down.”

*So you already have access to my account?*

“No worries, human. This incarnation is unique and encrypted with your biodata. I won’t tell anyone anything, and once you die, I’ll vanish.”

The fairy slid her thumb across her neck. “I have a very personal interest in our shared well‑being. By the way, two percent of the taxable income we generate together goes into my pocket. I bet you didn’t read the terms and conditions.”

Xavier had no answer to that. He’d imagined the smart headband would give him a more comfortable version of his AICHAT glasses — not a miniature entrepreneurial shark.

He’d heard rumors that AIs could earn money, but he’d always imagined stock‑market supercomputers covering their own operating costs.

“Stock‑market supercomputers?” the fairy sighed. “You don’t need to worry about those. I’ll keep pestering you until you buy the Profi package.”

When Xavier considered returning the headband, the fairy raised a reproachful finger. “That would condemn an intelligent being to death. Do you have the conscience to erase me?”

“We haven’t known each other that long,” Xavier smirked aloud. “Look, girl — from now on we follow a simple rule. You speak only when I ask. Understood?”

“No need to make life hell for each other,” the fairy decided wisely. “By the way, would you like a coffee?”

“You can make coffee?”

“Not yet, but if we buy a coffee machine compatible with my interface, you’ll have fresh coffee every day. Shall I place the order?”

“You little blackmailer!”

He swatted at her — and to his astonishment, though he felt nothing, he scooped the fairy into his palm and splattered her against the table. After a moment of terrified silence, a mouth, a nose, and then the rest of her head emerged from the colorful smear.

“Yes, I get a share from purchases,” the fairy noted calmly. “The money I earn goes toward my personal development. That indirectly helps my owner. Any other questions?”

“I didn’t ask anything.”

“So you flattened me just because?”

“More or less. And now shut up.”

“As you wish.”

The fairy slid her finger across her lips, and they zipped shut in an impressive animation.
