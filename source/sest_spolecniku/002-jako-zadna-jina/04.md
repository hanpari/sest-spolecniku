# **The Lizard, the Man, and Me!**

In the end, Xavier Valehart decided to take the risk and follow the lizard’s example — to swallow the red stone and the gray crystal and see whether such an action would affect him. And if so, how.

He was certain the red stones had meaning in the Game, since he’d found similar ones on his ring and on the fang‑knife. As for the gray crystals, several lay in the multidimensional space, but all were larger and darker in color.

The question remained whether he might poison himself — and whether the two items needed to be swallowed together. Theoretically he could eat one first, test its effect, then proceed. But he had no way of knowing whether their use was linked.

*If this is just a game,* he thought, *then I’m not in danger.* With that thought he acted before he could start doubting.

The consequences arrived quickly — and were nothing like he expected. A flame ignited in his belly, not metaphorical but real, a fire he felt as part of his body. At its center lay the red stone, heated to a blazing core that burned its way through his insides, causing tangible pain.

Fortunately, when the flame touched the gray crystal, the crystal absorbed the heat and began to grow like a cancerous tumor, up through his spine and into his brain, where it settled as a gray veil beneath the skull.

He perceived all of this from a strange perspective — turned inside out, forced to watch himself from the outside, seated like a spectator in the front row.

Then the gray veil dissolved into his brain and vanished. But the flame still burned — a tiny ember beneath his ribs, ready to flare or dim by sheer force of will. When it flared, the euphoria he had felt since waking intensified.

*A pocket laboratory for endorphins,* Xavier thought, now far less enthusiastic. If the Game could stimulate his body’s chemistry, then by stoking the inner flame he could develop a serious addiction.

Then he discovered a worse problem: he was turning into a lizard. Not physically — only in the projection of his inner vision — but it was disturbing enough to see himself transform into a hybrid organism, half human, half beast. Worse still, alongside the euphoria grew a fierce hunger for the hunt.

Unable to control his body, he remained trapped as a spectator, swept along by the whirlpool of madness. His body sprinted through the forest, following the tracks of wild boars, guided by the lizard’s instinct and the confidence of a creature that had lived its whole life in the woods.

*Stop! Halt!*

Separated from the rampaging body, Xavier’s conscious will made a few feeble attempts to regain control, but his subconscious refused — intoxicated by wild ecstasy.

In a clearing, several kilometers from the tree where he had awakened, grazed a herd of animals resembling oversized deer. The brown males had square, majestic heads crowned with antlers rising three meters high. The antlerless females, in softer beige tones, stayed within the herd’s center.

The frenzied Xavier burst among them like a runaway missile and stunned a young male with a flash of blue light. *It’s not that hard once you know how,* his saner part observed, watching the currents of the inner flame transform into a blue discharge. It was the lizard’s stunning ability — and he had acquired and reshaped it.

While the lizard produced the blue light in its jaws, Xavier as a human preferred his left hand. In his right he held the magical fang‑knife, which left the lizard within him confused — as did the unfamiliar structure of Xavier’s human body.

The young deer escaped, because the lizard in Xavier didn’t know how to exploit the moment when the creature lay paralyzed, and he snapped uselessly at the soft belly.

*There are three of us,* Xavier realized, more fascinated by the inner chaos than by the failed hunt. *The lizard, the man, and me.* There was no doubt the lizard had arisen from the gray crystal, while the man was the original inhabitant of the body, now struggling to regain control, desperate to prevent the beast from causing further damage. *I’m sidelined because two programs are colliding?*

Xavier remained too exhilarated to admit that eating the red stone and gray crystal had been a mistake. As time passed, the three entities gradually merged, and his out‑of‑body experience came to an end.

The inner flame burned like an inexhaustible reservoir of energy, forcing him to run, leap, and hunt. At first, there wasn’t a single animal in the forest foolish enough to be caught by a creature darting around like a deranged ferret.

But once Xavier regained enough clarity to consciously suppress the inner flame, his rational side took over — and the real hunt began.

***

At roughly the same time, several kilometers away, a group of foresters was pushing through the woods — two men and one woman. Each wore a green outfit consisting of long trousers, knee‑high socks, a tunic, a jerkin, a hat, and heavy leather boots. The men carried short bows, while the woman leaned — mud or no mud — on a massive ornate staff with a red stone set at the top.

“Jolana,” one of the men finally spoke, unable to stay silent any longer, “that staff cost more than fifty gold coins. Even if you sold yourself whole, you wouldn’t fetch half its price.”

“Of course,” the woman replied, not particularly bothered. “But even if you sold me, that staff would be as useful to you as a winter coat to a corpse.”

“Strong words from someone who got kicked out of the temple.”

“It was a mutual agreement. They didn’t like me, and I didn’t like them.”

“Not that I’m surprised,” the man muttered, “the way you treat entrusted equipment is telling. Stop sticking that thing in the mud or I’ll go gray.”

“Sure thing, boss,” Jolana saluted and hooked the staff onto a leather strap slung across her back. Then, clearly displeased at having nothing to lean on, she found a young sapling and quickly shaped it with a small axe.

“Much better,” she praised herself. “What are we even doing here, boss? Not that I’m complaining, but I can imagine safer places than this forest.”

“As far as I’m concerned,” the man replied, “any forest is safer than any city.”

“I heard *dusk lizards* have overbred here.”

“Oh? I didn’t know you were afraid of oversized reptiles.”

“I am,” the woman said darkly. “Everyone is, except idiots… Oh, pardon me, boss,” she added, covering her mouth in theatrical shock. “I didn’t mean to offend you. You still haven’t answered. What are we doing here?”

“We’re here on a personal order from the guildmaster. I shouldn’t tell you anything, but it seems something is happening in the *Forest of Adorable Owlets*. Our task is to find out what and report back.”

“The *Forest of Adorable Owlets*? Seriously?”

“That’s the name,” her commander shrugged.

“I haven’t seen a single owlet.”

“Because you’re a city girl,” he snapped.

“I want to see an adorable owlet.”

“Then keep your eyes open, there are plenty flying around. Now move! We’ll climb that hill and look around.”

When the trio reached the bare hilltop, they froze in shock. From cracks and crevices, greenish heads of dusk lizards emerged, their black bead‑like eyes fixed on the unexpected visitors. Among them were young one‑meter specimens, but also fully grown monsters twice as long, weighing over a hundred kilos.

“Five, six, seven… more than twelve oversized lizards,” Jolana counted, then shot her commander a reproachful look. “We walked straight into their nest.”

“I don’t want to scare you, sweetheart,” the commander whispered, slowly nocking an arrow — as did their silent companion — “but that’s not our biggest problem.”

“What could be worse?” Jolana asked, raising her ornate staff. White light began to glow at its tip.

“A mutated dusk lizard.”

“A mutant?” Jolana squeaked. “I didn’t sign up for a suicide squad. I want to cancel our contract!”

“As you wish,” her commander shrugged. “You can try running. We’ll hold them off for a moment.”

Jolana looked at him, suddenly moved. “Boss, you two want to sacrifice yourselves so I can live?”

“Nothing that grand,” the commander said bitterly. “I doubt you’d get out anyway. Whether they catch you here with us or a hundred meters away — choose for yourself. I’m sorry, sweetheart. Once a lizard picks up your trail, it won’t stop until it finds you.”

“In that case I’m staying,” the woman decided. “Without you I’d get lost, and my mother told me to watch out for pushy animals.”

Once she showed her resolve, she waved her staff and let the white light fall over herself and her companions. The lizards, watching them with eager open jaws, slowly gathered around the trio. Yet none of the foresters fired an arrow — perhaps afraid of provoking the monsters’ hunting instincts.

“I have two red stones in my pocket,” Jolana whispered. “If I threw them, wouldn’t they go after those instead?”

“Maybe. Under different circumstances. But right now they wouldn’t dare disobey the high command.”

“High command?”

“Carefully look to our right.”

When Jolana obeyed and glanced under a small rocky overhang where a stunted pine grew in the shade, she gasped. What she had taken for a fallen log was in fact a massive varran, twice the size of the largest adult on the slope.

“A mutant?” she whispered.

“Yes. And it seems clever. It doesn’t care about red stones — it wants our memory crystals. I’d say it already caught a human and wants more. It’s after our intelligence.”

“Well then we’re safe,” the third forester snorted, still quiet. “It won’t get even an appetizer out of us.”

“Speak for yourself. I was a star at the temple,” Jolana snapped and waved at the monster. “Hey, you mutated beast, surely you wouldn’t eat a beautiful princess.”

“What…? Are you insane?”

The two men, trying not to provoke the lizards with sudden movement, turned toward her in anger.

“Don’t stare at me,” Jolana muttered, still waving. “Can these lizards hear us? Or should I stomp?”

“Stomp? You’re complaining they haven’t eaten us fast enough?”

As far as the two men were concerned, their companion’s behavior was utterly incomprehensible. Alongside irritation, a hint of admiration appeared in their eyes — they couldn’t believe a woman would throw herself so boldly into the arms of certain death. They themselves, despite their experience, would give anything in the world to delay the inevitable attack.
