# Don’t you think you’re from the cheap end of town

Getting into a fight with Xantipa Millerová was like getting into a fight with a rabid hammer factory. Her fists crashed into Xavier with tireless regularity, without pause and without a shred of imagination. Only the monotony of her attacks allowed Xavier to endure the first onslaught.

Fortunately for him, being a *multiple champion* didn’t mean much. In practice, silvers like Xantipa Millerová triumphed thanks to technological superiority: bones reinforced with nanomaterials didn’t break, not even when fragile finger joints slammed into an opponent’s face; supertonic drinks — miraculous elixirs of youth, strength, and endurance (with equally staggering price tags) — could turn a person into something close to a superhero, and virtual gyms tailored fighting styles to each customer.

That was why bronzes, let alone plastics, didn’t bother challenging above their rank. They knew they had no chance. The slogan society bombarded them with — **“It’s all up to you!”** — carried a bitter aftertaste of impossibility. While system proponents flaunted a few lucky individuals who managed to climb to the golden peak, no one mentioned the millions who succumbed to VIUR every year.

Had a bronze stood in Xavier’s place, he would have ended up with shattered bones within moments. Xantipa Millerová was about as subtle as a steam hammer. She relied on a relentless offensive that left her opponent beaten into paste.

“Damn beast,” someone shouted, half admiring, half disgusted.

“Quiet, she’ll hear you,” another voice warned. Public fights like this helped cultivate a sense of inferiority — a socially useful conviction that everyone had earned their citizen rank fairly.

“Go on! Finish the Shmuck.”

Although Xantipa Millerová couldn’t win the crowd’s affection, there were still a few onlookers — especially young women — who saw her as an ideal worth following. Perhaps those girls imagined themselves in her place, blessed with a perfect body and indestructible confidence.

Xavier defended patiently, adjusting to her pace. As a silver, he had enough advantages on his side to withstand the furious assault. On the contrary, Xantipa frowned in irritation when a venomous counterstrike nearly knocked the breath out of her.

She likely couldn’t explain why she wasn’t gaining the upper hand. Even Xavier was surprised by how quickly he adapted to her style.

*Maybe I should thank the charlatan Blacskheart,* he thought, bewildered. Instead of collapsing under his long‑term insomnia, he felt better and better. Most likely because of the painful procedure he had undergone at the **Game Center**.

“That’s impossible!” Xantipa Millerová screamed in rage. Thick veins bulged on her forehead as she whipped herself into a frenzy. As a silver, she could regulate her metabolism to a degree and briefly push her physical limits.

“Too early,” Xavier smiled. Every seasoned fighter saved their trump cards for last. But Xantipa Millerová was driven by the urge to prove her superiority. One of her kicks struck a concrete pillar by the roadside and shattered it in an explosion of fragments.

Several painful cries rose from the onlookers. Xavier jumped back quickly. If the woman had hit him, she would likely have crippled him.

*Victory at any cost,* he thought grimly. *Even if she killed me, she’d shrug and say I wasn’t good enough.*

“Don’t you want to give up?” Medard shouted, terrified. “She’s completely insane!”

“Hey, bettors!” voices echoed from all sides. “Can we still bet on the Bitch?”

“Of course, but only five to one against her. No, wait — seven to one.”

The nervous bookmaker probably regretted offering such favorable odds earlier. What was happening before him was a clear massacre.

“I’ll take it!” someone yelled. “Seventy thousand on the Bitch. Ten thousand is good money.”

“Please, bet responsibly,” the man groaned. “But whatever must happen, let it happen. Anyone else want to bet on the favorite?”

“Here’s my wager. Watch him — don’t let him run off with the money.”

“I’m an honest man,” the bookmaker wailed. “A guarantee of reliability. Do you want to ruin me?”

Although he kept accepting bets, the bookmaker smiled under his moustache. He didn’t seem troubled by what was happening in the improvised ring. If Xavier had the time, he would have bet on himself as well. At this moment, he was certain he would win — unless he made a mistake.

Most of the fights Xantipa had ever participated in must have been against weaker opponents. That was why she underestimated Xavier and ended up in a tactical battle she had no chance of winning. Fatigue began to show; her movements slowed. The adrenaline storm demanded its toll, and the woman retreated into defense.

Someone noticed and shouted: “Five thousand on Shmuck! He’s my boy!”

“Sure,” the bookmaker sneered. “But the odds have changed. It’s now two to one *against* Shmuck.”

“What?”

The onlookers turned toward him in surprise. Even Medard, standing nearby and unable to read the subtle signs of the shifting situation, quietly asked, “Why would you do that, sir?”

The man tapped his nose slyly. “Intuition, dear sir. Pure intuition.” Then he added quietly, “That woman has not only the strength of an ox, but also its brain. Whereas your friend is a damn dark horse. Would you convince him to work for me?”

“Work? A silver? For you?”

Medard was stunned by the audacity with which the bookmaker dared aim so high.

“Don’t be so shocked, dear sir. Here’s my card. Clifford Oplatka, that’s my name. Independent bookmaker.”

Medard examined the card. It looked solid — far more solid than its owner. It even listed a company address and a website.

“You were lucky to stumble upon this.”

“Luck has nothing to do with it,” the man replied, almost offended. “I’d be a poor professional if I didn’t recognize Valehart. He has a certain reputation in our circles, you know. Secretive, perhaps; database wipes, certainly. But in our trade, we must know even those who fly under the radar.”

“You’ve met him before?”

“No. But I know who he is. He hasn’t been very active lately, but a year ago he supposedly had a hand in the troubles at **First-Savings**.”

“That’s nonsense. How could one silver cause trouble for a corporation?”

Clifford Oplatka rolled his eyes. “I’m not saying Valehart's worked alone. I’m only interested in the fact that your friend is a capable duelist, and together we could make good money. If you give him my card and he calls me, you get ten. If we strike a deal, you get a hundred.”

Medard still remembered vividly how Xavier mocked him for not going after what he wanted.

“Don’t you think you’re from the cheap end of town?” he asked.

“What? I won’t give more than ten thousand for a phone call. But if you convince Valehart, we can talk about a hundred and fifty thousand.”

“Ehm?”

When the stunned Medard realized Oplatka was speaking in thousands, he lost his voice. The social benefits he received as a plastic citizen didn’t even reach three thousand a month.

“One would think that in your situation you’d jump at it like a hen at a worm,” Oplatka remarked, turning back to watch the fight.

“In my situation?”

“You’re a plastic without vaccination, right? If you declare an annual income over two hundred thousand, you’ve got bronze in the bag.”

“Why would I be, ehm, a plastic without vaccination?”

“Oh come on! You’re good! You’ve got the badge under your shirt. Every bronze wears it so everyone can see. And judging by your belly, I know you’ve already given up the rat race. When were you last in a gym, hmm?”

Feeling ashamed, Medard turned away. “If there’s a chance, I’ll give him your card. But I won’t persuade him.”

“Your mistake. I’m not forcing you,” Oplatka said, then pointed at the two fighters with professional interest. “It’ll be over soon. No surprises.”

But here Clifford Oplatka was wrong. When Xantipa Millerová realized she had hit a wall she couldn’t break, she hissed quietly, “I’ll buy a draw.”

Since silvers generally couldn’t afford to lose, such offers were common. Naturally, *fair play* suffered, but the end justified the means.

Xavier feigned a strike and whispered, “How much?”

“Two hundred.”

“Too little.”

His opponent nearly spat blood. *Two hundred thousand dollars is too little?*

“I was right,” she growled. “You’re a bastard who only cares about money.”

“We don’t have to agree,” Xavier replied indifferently. He felt no sympathy for her — and if he could squeeze out all her savings, he’d do it with a smile. She was a silver, a dangerous predator who had clawed her way up through sheer ruthlessness.

“Three hundred,” she snarled.

When he didn’t answer, she added almost desperately, “I don’t have more.”

“I’ll take supertonics too.”

“You’re insane!”

Supertonic drinks were allocated based on citizen rank. The truly effective ones couldn’t be bought with money; even silvers lacked them and remained dependent on golds who could procure them. People like Xantipa Millerová considered genuine loyalty a burden and proudly went wherever the highest profit beckoned. Money eventually stopped mattering to them, so the elite encouraged their dependence on stimulants.

“Young Alfred will get you more,” Xavier hissed. “If you lose, he’ll kick you out.”

“Two drinks and the money! I have no more and I won’t give more.”

Even if she was lying, Xavier was satisfied. He jumped back and shouted loudly, “How about a draw, madam?”

His move surprised her. By offering the draw himself, he admitted he was the weaker side. Even if her superior learned about the duel, he couldn’t blame her — except perhaps for not crushing her opponent for the corporation’s glory.

“Why not,” she said coldly, hiding her relief behind indifferent eyes. “As I said, I don’t have time to waste on every provocateur. You’re lucky, sir.”

Then she turned and quietly walked to her belongings. She pulled paper tissues from her handbag and, with the help of the vendor she had earlier made her witness, wiped off dirt and blood. If she felt anything like Xavier, she must have felt run over by a steamroller, yet her movements remained calm, as if returning from a light workout not worth mentioning. Her opponent didn’t bother maintaining a proud façade — he collapsed into the grass with loud groans, drinking small cups of water Medard brought from the electronics shop.

The onlookers — especially those who had used the *in promptu* betting service — realized what the unexpected turn meant.

“What! And our money? Do we get it back?”

With a look of infinite sorrow, Clifford Oplatka stretched his foxlike face and said mournfully, “Your bets are void, friends. All of them. The game is the game.”

Some refused to accept it easily.

“Do you even have a license, you cheat?” someone shouted. “If you don’t return the money, we’ll catch you and report you for illegal business!”

“You don’t need to catch me, friends. I’ll gladly wait here with you for the police. And I’ll happily hand out my business cards. My trade is perfectly honest, I pay taxes, and I’m an upright bronze.”

After he showed them his badge, no one insisted further. The crowd dispersed with grumbling. Clifford Oplatka also left — but not before giving Medard a meaningful wink, gesturing with thumb and pinky that he expected a phone call.
