# **Why are you trying to insult me?**

When the girl finally agreed to talk, Xavier Valehart hit a snag. He had originally intended to lead her to a corner where, as he discovered, nearby shops dumped their trash — cardboard scraps, tape, and fruit peels littered the ground. *So much for romance,* he thought, and instead headed toward the shade of a tall linden tree, which offered a more suitable setting for a conversation with a charming Samaritan. Perhaps under the impression that this was a confidential matter, Svetlana Troyak stayed close to him, barely half a meter away.

“I don’t want you to think you owe me anything,” she said, “but I’m willing to pay you if you tell me the secret of how to get into the Game.”

“Really? I thought you didn’t care about the Game.”

“Of course not. I have more important things to do than run around hunting monsters.”

“I’m sure. Shopping takes a lot of time.”

Xavier let his gaze travel from her head to her feet. From the open high‑heeled sandals, to the white pants and blouse with cooling elements (an essential and expensive item in the scorching summer), to the delicate gold bracelet with analog watch hands, to the skin enhanced by cosmetic creams and effective supplements.

“You’re an idiot. I don’t judge you by your looks either. I heard them shouting at you that you’re a shmuck.”

*Touché!* Why do you want into the Game?”

“Because my brother is a dangerous lunatic.”

“Then he should be locked in an asylum.”

“He’s not dangerous to others — only to himself.”

“You like playing psychiatrist, don’t you?” Xavier laughed. “I suffer from paranoia, your brother is a madman too. If that’s not a diagnosis, then — as Mr. Clifford Oplatka offered — I’ll eat my smelly socks.”

But Svetlana wasn’t rattled. “My brother wants to become a famous duelist.”

“Then he’s a madman,” Xavier agreed. “Is that why he carries that wooden sword?”

“If only that were all! I could list every martial art he’s ever been interested in. He’s obsessed. He keeps watching those disgusting fights on TV — you know which ones. The ones where people punch each other until they’re a bloody pulp.”

“I know them.”

“But you don’t enjoy them, do you?”

“I may look a bit worn out, but I still have some taste left. I’m on your side.”

“Those poor fools don’t realize regenerative injections can’t perform miracles. I’ve seen what’s left of them in the hospital after they take too many blows to the head. Shaking wrecks who can’t even pour themselves a glass of water. Understand, I don’t want him to end up like that.”

“He could try *chess* or *go*.”

“That’s beyond our intellectual range.”

“Excuse me?”

“My brother and I don’t have abstract thinking developed at the expense of other parts of the brain.”

“You’re saying you’re kind, but not smart?”

“Why are you trying to insult me? Of course we’re not stupid, but we have no chance of reaching the higher league. I tried steering him that way once. Our best result was placing in the top twenty at a junior tournament.”

“I take it back. You’re kind and smart. So the Game is another way to redirect your brother?”

“Yes. In virtual reality he’s in no danger.”

“Hm.”

*Is the Game truly harmless?* Xavier knew nothing about the technology itself, but the more he learned, the more treacherous it seemed. Escaping into virtual reality had always been the domain of plastics and bronzes, who at least in simulation achieved what the real world denied them. They became superhumans, rulers of lands and empires, seductive women and daring men. Silvers and golds admitted gaming as a weakness, a bad habit they should abandon.

“I understand your father doesn’t agree with your plan.”

“You understand correctly,” Svetlana said seriously. “Our father suffers from prejudices, just like Mr. Medard. Though not in all areas,” she added with a hint of indignation. “In a way he has something in common with you. He also can’t stop thinking about her.”

*Her?*

“We’re talking about Gabrielle?”

“Yes. Is she attractive to older men? I don’t want to sound conceited, but in a beauty contest I’d have the edge over her.”

Xavier wondered whether Svetlana truly wanted to compare herself, or whether she was speaking on behalf of her mother, judging by the remark about her father’s inclinations. *Perhaps we all suffer from prejudices,* he sighed. Svetlana and her mother likely couldn’t understand why anyone would be interested in an orphan from a poor family. Genetics were one thing, but most of female beauty came from modern treatments available only to the privileged. Silvers and golds almost without exception invested in their appearance and that of their children so everyone could recognize them even on the street. When Gabrielle and Svetlana stood side by side, the first looked like an unedited original, while the second like a perfect retouch. And he had no doubt the same applied to Svetlana’s mother.

“I’m interested in her,” Xavier said, “because she reminds me of a woman who shaped me in a fundamental way.”

When Svetlana blushed, he added calmly, “Not in the way you imagine. Perhaps your father’s intentions are innocent too.”

“Him! You don’t know the situation in our family, and I have no need to explain anything to you.”

Yet she continued, perhaps because she needed to vent. Xavier Valehart’s sense of propriety rebelled. *This girl should have more sense than to confide in a stranger on the street.*

“He brought her half a year ago to introduce Gabrielle to us. She’s a year younger than we are, but according to him everyone considers her a genius child. As for me…” Svetlana hesitated.

“You think that her abstract thinking developed at the expense of other parts of the brain,” Xavier quoted, firmly determined to lecture her about safety — but not before she told him more about Gabrielle.

“I don’t want to wrong her. In my opinion she doesn’t realize at all what tension she causes in our family. Mother is on the verge of collapse. She’s completely dependent on father and terrified she’ll lose everything.”

Xavier was certain Gabrielle *did* realize the tension in the Troyak family — and probably contributed to it. He had no intention of sympathizing with Svetlana’s mother either. Her daughter understood perfectly well that parents defended their selfish interests. None of them cared about keeping the family together for the children — as Svetlana selfishly imagined.

“So you’re trying to get her out of the house? I found it strange that you two would be friends.”

“But we *are* friends! Gabrielle is amazing intellectually. If father had adopted her, as he claimed from the beginning, no one would have a problem — not even Mother. But things developed completely differently. Father keeps trying to be alone with her. It’s absolutely disgusting. They all seem so rotten to me.”

“So?”

“I want to be independent,” Svetlana explained. “I’m not as naive as everyone thinks. I understand what Mother fears. I understand why Father thinks he has a right to Gabrielle. In today’s twisted world, plenty of people would applaud him. The only thing I care about is my brother — and surprisingly, her.”

“You modestly left yourself out.”

Even if Xavier decided to admire her, he couldn’t ignore how much Svetlana prided herself on her determination to sacrifice herself for others. It was, in its own way, fanaticism — a personal rebellion against a corrupt society. *I, who will lead by example and collect wounded doves along the way.* She and Medard resembled each other. While Medard believed his tendency to do good limited him, Svetlana wasn’t ashamed and marched against the current with her head proudly raised. Both were infuriating.

“I won’t meddle in your family,” he said coldly. “As for the Game, I don’t want more than I’ve already received. Treatment and information. What I tell you stays between us.”

“No, it doesn’t,” she shook her head quickly. “I can’t promise I won’t tell my brother or her. If that’s a problem, we shouldn’t continue.”

*She truly isn’t naive! She quickly spotted the trap I was about to set.* It had been a small bit of mischief on his part — a minor moral dilemma she was supposed to wrestle with. Reveal the secret for herself despite her promise.

“Very well,” he grumbled. “But what about those two?”

“They won’t tell anyone,” she declared confidently. “You have my word.”

“If corporate lawyers knock on my door, you’ll answer for it.”

“You’re threatening me?”

“Yes.”

“But I think you’re basically a good person,” she noted. “You won’t hurt me. Just like you didn’t hurt that silver woman,” she added with a remarkable hint of disapproval. “Why did you let her go?”

“We’re talking about Xantipa Miller now?” he asked. “You have a remarkable habit of jumping from topic to topic.” He let the threats drop — threatening Svetlana as if she were a street thug felt wrong.

“Yes, her. It’s damn unfair, if you want my opinion. If you were playing chess, she wouldn’t be allowed to keep her smart headband so she wouldn’t have an advantage. But here she stepped in with reinforced bones. She kicked a concrete post apart. If she faced a bronze or a plastic, she’d kill him.”

Svetlana was likely imagining her brother in Xavier’s place — hence her indignation.

“Our world isn’t fair,” Xavier said expansively. “A moment ago you compared yourself to Gabrielle. With her background, she hardly can outshine you - the competion between you two will never be fair.”

She wrinkled her nose in disagreement but didn’t let herself be provoked. “You should have eliminated her,” she declared. “No matter how peaceful I am, I wouldn’t pity that Xantipa.”

*The problem is you’re not peaceful — you’re fanatical.* A vision flashed in his mind: Svetlana as a modern Joan of Arc marching against heretics, long golden hair billowing before a pyre of sinners of the modern age.

“Back to the Game,” he said. “Listen carefully…”

He told her the same thing he had told Medard. Medard had been terrified by the mention of the torture chair, while she accepted it with a nod. Perhaps she suspected what it was — perhaps she had even tried a milder version. She asked several questions he couldn’t answer. It seemed Svetlana had some awareness of how the gaming technology worked — or at least pretended to, so she wouldn’t lose face as a medical student.

Then they returned to the others, where the two groups said their goodbyes. Xantipa Miller had vanished, and Xavier could continue shopping for the smart headband undisturbed. The salesman who had acted as her second was proud of the fact (once everything was over and she was safely gone) and treated Xavier with cold politeness, unwilling to side with the enemy faction. The terror that he had nearly lost his bronze badge because of that woman had faded, replaced by devotion to someone so impressive.
