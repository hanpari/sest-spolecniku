# Adoption in SIXBOUND

One of the many controversial themes explored in SIXBOUND is adoption. For most people in our world, adoption is associated with either selfless or self-interested care for orphans and abandoned children. Like all charitable acts, adoption carries an aura of nobility, yet beneath the surface there are often other motives, far less admirable.

In SIXBOUND, adoption gives rise to a rather paradoxical situation. Xavier and Lydia, citizens of the second caste known as Silvers, decide to adopt twenty-year-old Gabrielle Marrow. Lydia thus becomes the mother of an adult daughter at the age of thirty, while Xavier does the same at thirty-six.

Within a society we have previously described as dystopian, so as not to offend the sensitivities of today's readers too greatly, this is considered entirely normal.

It is tacitly assumed that citizens of the second caste, who form the productive backbone of their world, have neither the time nor the inclination to waste their best years raising children. Such an effort is regarded as unnecessary when there is an abundance of motivated orphans left behind by the victims of recurring viral epidemics.

Competition among these orphans is fierce. The most ambitious and talented believe that adoption offers a path to social advancement. Gabrielle is one such orphan.

From the perspective of society, this arrangement is both natural and advantageous. Silver parents may never produce children of their own, but they can pass on their legacy, including their social status, to a suitable candidate who meets every standard expected of a citizen of the second caste. Such an orphan is typically highly productive, results-oriented, and, by modern standards, a functional sociopath.

This is one of the key distinctions between citizens of the first and second castes. While the former believe in the natural superiority of their genetic material, the latter are obsessed with measurable metrics. Lydia herself, after all, is the daughter of one of her Gold father's many concubines.

Only after Silvers ascend and become Golds do they come to believe that they have both the right and the duty to pass on their own genes and raise biological children. From this perspective, Xavier's description of the Silver caste as voluntary eunuchs is surprisingly accurate.

Naturally, these rules are not absolute, and not every Silver follows them. Although the society they inhabit regards conspicuous asexuality as a mark of success and a higher ideal, sexual desire cannot be suppressed so easily. Silvers may take lovers from lower social castes, or they may simply start families of their own. The pressure is social rather than legal.

Nor does society have any explicit interest in preventing Silvers from reproducing. After all, these are its most capable individuals, and if members of other castes are permitted to reproduce, it would be wasteful not to preserve their genes as well.

Silver men and women therefore place their own children into foreign nests, like cuckoos, or more precisely into someone else's womb. Volunteer mothers, for whom motherhood and the upbringing of citizen-candidates provide both income and, more importantly, protection from newly released viral strains, receive Silver children in the form of fertilized cells.

Although it is never stated outright in SIXBOUND, several such cuckoo chicks appear throughout the story. Among the Corsairs, notable examples include Eliza Crowe and a young man known only as Bin.

This raises an obvious question: once these cuckoo children reach adulthood, would Silvers not seek them out and claim them as heirs?

In practice, this happens only rarely. Favoring one's own biological offspring, however unremarkable, when one could instead adopt a more ambitious and accomplished candidate from a wider pool is considered inefficient.

As you can see, this creates a separation between genetic inheritance and individual potential. Silvers do not indulge in sentimentality, and their choices are expected, at least in theory, to remain entirely pragmatic.
