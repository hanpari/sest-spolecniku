# **The One Who Walks the Shadows**

When Xavier fell asleep, Lydia threw herself into creating a new avatar. This time she was offered a choice between a doll, a green furry caterpillar, and a hooded figurine. None of the options thrilled her.

“Why am I so unlucky?” she complained aloud toward the little fairy, who watched her comfortably seated on the porch of a miniature dollhouse.

“It’s not about appearance, silver lady,” the fairy said. “It’s an expression of your deep subconscious. In a way,” she added sharply, “you’ve improved. Last time you ended up with a stupid mutt.”

“So I want to be a sexy Barbie?”

“Maybe. Or you feel like a disgusting caterpillar waiting to hatch into a beautiful butterfly.”

The fairy said it so doubtfully that Lydia felt insulted.

“What about the last option?”

“A mysterious ninja? Who knows! I refuse to waste computational capacity on your psychological profile. Try dissecting yourself. You humans enjoy that. You’ve flooded the internet with your pointless problems.”

*Pointless problems?*

Lydia remembered her mother lying by the pool, sipping a cocktail, listening with bored patience to her daughter’s lectures about how everyone must be useful to society. *Becoming a gold‑nose concubine is one solution,* she thought with contempt. *But I haven’t sunk that low yet.*

With a decisive gesture she pushed the doll aside and focused on the caterpillar.

“Will it really turn into a butterfly?”

“In time, certainly,” the fairy said. “You humans need the illusion of progress. You spend millions on cosmetic changes.”

The bitter memory of the money she had wasted on treats and accessories for her dog made Lydia shudder.

“No, thank you,” she snapped, turning to the last figurine. The avatar, wrapped in pitch‑black cloth with only two large dark eyes peeking out, calmly returned her gaze. “And what are you supposed to be?”

“I am a ninja — the one who walks the shadows,” the avatar answered in the quiet voice of a teenage boy. “I solve problems. Forever.”

He punctuated his words with a sharp motion. A throwing knife shot from his hand and buried itself in the virtual undergrowth. A loud scream echoed, followed by ominous silence.

“Incredible!”

The little fairy burst into laughter so hard she fell off her garden chair. Irritated, Lydia wished she could strangle her. *Someone kill her already!*

“As you command, my lady.”

The little ninja bowed deeply and sank into the floor. A moment later he appeared behind the fairy, katana raised. Before he could strike, the fairy lazily flicked her wand, and a giant hammer materialized above him, pounding him into the floor like a nail.

The crushed warrior crawled out of the hole and brushed dirt off his trousers. “I have failed,” he said in a grave voice. “But I swear that one day I will bring you her head, my lady.”

“Can you train him?” Lydia asked doubtfully.

“A manifestation of suppressed aggressive tendencies,” the fairy noted approvingly. “Good material to work with… However,” she added, fixing Lydia with a stern look, “you also have to try.”

“Rest assured, I won’t spoil my second avatar.”

“I’m talking about something else, silver lady. You’ve been carrying a treasure in your briefcase for a week. Isn’t it time to open it?”

*A treasure?*

When Lydia opened her ruined leather briefcase and laid out the pierced documents, only a cardboard box with the HC logo remained inside.

“This?”

“What else?”

“Should I log in right here and now?”

*In a stranger’s apartment, with a strange man sleeping beside me?* she added silently. Remembering how poorly Valehart tolerated her presence, she felt awkward and out of place. Like him, she was used to the privacy of her own home.

“At least you’ll have an excuse to stay,” the fairy smirked.

“An excuse? I don’t need an excuse.”

“Of course. In that case, thank you — the door is right there.”

*She knows I don’t want to be alone,* Lydia realized. *I have no friends I can trust. My acquaintances worship the selfish philosophy of society.*

Avatars knew only the thoughts of their owners, but Lydia believed the little tormentor could read her like a book. *I want someone to help me. Someone who still has a heart.*

She blushed when she remembered how contemptuously she had reacted to the fairy’s unexpected compassion when she was about to delete her virtual dog.

*If Valehart had nothing to do with my attack,* she thought, *then he’s the only one whose heart might still be moved by my situation.*

With bitter disgust she remembered Jacob Lickboothe. She had worked with him for over a year on a university project and considered him a friend. Yet he had done nothing but scream helplessly when her life was in danger.

“No worries,” the fairy said, pointing at the devastated ninja. “If you leave Shadowstep on guard, he can always call the police. Not that my master has any personal interest in you,” she added proudly. “But once you’re in the Game, maybe you’ll convince him to look after you.”

“Ridiculous,” the ninja said. “You cannot give me names.”

“I’ll be your trainer,” the fairy huffed. “I’ll be your instructor. That’s what I’m paid for. So far you haven’t impressed me.”

Grateful that no one was paying attention to her, Lydia thought: *Valehart really does put emphasis on the Game. But that could be just another trick.*

When she opened the transmitter box, she noticed an identical one lying beside Xavier’s bed.

“Will you guard me, uh… Shadowstep?” she asked uncertainly.

“You may rely on me, my lady.”

With an uneasy feeling she might be making a mistake, Lydia lay down on the living‑room couch and locked the glass door behind her.

*Maybe I should wait,* she thought as she drifted off. *Why enter the Game when someone wants to kill me in the real world?*

…

Outside, the wind whistled and leaves rustled.

She woke in a cabin decorated with ornate carvings in rare wood. Lydia, who lived in a plastic world, looked around in awe and ran her fingers over the smooth surface of a writing desk, surprised by her sharpened senses, unblunted by virtual reality.

*What happened here? A disaster?*

The cabin bore signs of catastrophe. There were two beds: a small, plain one where Lydia lay, and a grand canopy bed reduced to splinters — some force had crushed the wall and everything in its path. On the white sheet dried blood remained, but no body, only strange stones she brushed aside.

The door burst open and a man rushed in. “You’re awake, miss? We’ve got trouble!”

“You don’t know how to knock,” she snapped, more startled than frightened. *This is the Game,* she reminded herself. *I’m not in real danger.*

But she wasn’t sure. The stranger, whom she assumed was a computer‑generated character, felt disturbingly real. Dressed in an ornate uniform, he looked like a young soldier of low rank — more decorative than functional.

“Forgive me, miss, I didn’t mean to disturb your mourning.”

“Mourning?”

“Your poor lady,” he pointed at the bloody bed. “I believe she didn’t suffer.”

*And I believe I don’t care,* Lydia thought. She was startled by her own reaction. Naturally, she had no reason to weep over a virtual tragedy, but cynical remarks weren’t her style.

“What happened?”

The soldier gave her a look that was half sympathetic, half annoyed. “We crashed. Something attacked us while we were flying over this cursed forest. Only you and I survived. It’s been a week,” he added, as if suggesting Lydia should have accepted the situation by now.

*A week? I stopped by the **Game Center** a week ago. Was the Game waiting for me to log in?*

She understood the unspoken reproach.

“I’m sorry,” she said, standing up firmly. The floor tilted beneath her, wood creaking and cracking.

“We’re stuck between the trees,” the soldier said. “Be careful.”

*What kind of ship flies over a forest and crashes between trees?*

They walked down a slanted corridor, leaning against inlaid walls while broken glass crunched underfoot.

*Am I a maid?* Lydia thought, trapped in a bodice and puffed skirts. *A servant?*

She had never played virtual games — her father considered them a waste of time — but she knew players imagined fun differently.

“Careful!” the soldier shouted as the floor lurched.

“Ow,” she slipped and fell into the shards, palms first. Pain shot through her hands, tears sprang to her eyes, and blood immediately welled up.

“Holy heavens! Please be careful.”

His restrained politeness masked irritation. *I must be getting on his nerves,* Lydia thought. *He has his own worries. I’m a burden.*

Still, he took her hands and carefully pulled out the shards from beneath the peeled skin.

“At least you’re not crying,” he smiled. “You have delicate hands. Crashing with such a beauty was always my dream.”

*He’s terrified,* she realized. Beneath the smile she sensed palpable fear. It was contagious. Danger in the Game was real. The pulsing pain proved that injury — or death — would be horrifying.

“What’s happening?” she asked, pulling away. “I don’t need a caretaker.”

Fascinated by the pain, she didn’t notice her body moving and speaking without her will. A flame ignited under her ribs, reaching toward her wounded fingers, closing the open cuts with short spasms.

“I just… You… How can you…?”

“Let’s go,” the unknown entity in Lydia’s body snapped, annoyed by his awe. “Don’t ask and I won’t lie. What’s the problem?”

“I think vultures are coming.”

“Vultures?”

“Criminals,” the soldier said grimly. “I bet they’re after the ship’s cargo. And I doubt they’ll leave witnesses.”
